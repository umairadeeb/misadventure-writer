# Humor Playbook

Techniques for making a travel post funny. All examples are invented for illustration. Use the techniques, not the jokes.

## 1. Self-deprecation is the default target

The narrator is the idiot. The place is fine. The locals were helpful, the train was on time, the map was correct, and the narrator still managed to end up in a laundry room holding a baguette.

Why it works: readers forgive a narrator who is the butt of the joke, and they trust the sincere moments more afterward. The reverse (mocking the place) reads as snobbery and curdles fast.

Test: if you swap in a different traveler, does the joke still work? If yes, it's a generic joke. Funny posts depend on this particular narrator's particular failure.

- Weak: "The train system was confusing."
- Better: "The ticket machine offered me four languages and I failed in all of them."

Self-deprecation must be earned from real events. Do not invent incompetence the notes don't support. Exaggerate a real mishap; don't fabricate one.

Avoid the pity version. "I'm so bad at everything" repeated is not a joke, it's a mood. Pick the specific failure.

## 2. Specificity beats adjectives

"Funny" is a claim. A detail is evidence. Replace the claim with the detail.

- Weak: "The goat was weird."
- Better: "A goat looked at me with open contempt and then ate my map."

The ladder, from weakest to strongest:
1. Announce the joke ("It was hilarious")
2. Add an adjective ("a ridiculous hat")
3. Name the thing ("a hat shaped like a traffic cone")
4. Name the thing and one absurd, true behavior ("a traffic-cone hat, worn with total confidence by a man selling a single lemon")

Numbers, brand names, exact times, and small physical facts are worth more than any adjective. If the notes don't contain them, ask the user. Don't invent them.

## 3. Understate the big, overstate the tiny

Switch the volume. React calmly to the enormous thing and with full alarm to the trivial thing.

- Understate: "The bus left without me. This was fine. It had my luggage."
- Overstate: "A pigeon landed on my table and I had to leave the continent."

Pick the pairing that matches the narrator's real temperament (from the voice profile). A naturally dry narrator mostly understates; a naturally anxious one mostly overstates.

## 4. Callbacks and the running bit

Plant a detail early, forget about it, and bring it back late with a twist. Aim for one running bit per post. Two is a lot. Three is a sketch show.

Good running bits come from the real notes: an object, a phrase a companion kept saying, a recurring failure. If nothing recurs, don't manufacture one. Use a single callback instead.

Mechanics:
- Plant it casually, in a sentence that is not the joke ("I had bought a bread roll for emergencies").
- Let at least two or three paragraphs pass.
- Pay it off when the reader has half-forgotten: change its meaning, escalate it, or invert it ("The emergency finally arrived. The roll had been in my bag for six days and could now be used in construction.").
- Never explain a callback. If you have to say "remember the bread roll?" it failed.

## 5. Brochure versus reality

Set the expectation, then show what happened. The gap is the joke.

- "The website promised a serene lakeside retreat. The lake was a pond. The retreat was a man named after a beer brand yelling at a lawnmower."

Keep the brochure half grounded in something the user actually saw or read, such as a booking page, a sign, or a friend's recommendation. Don't mock a real, named small business; mock the gap between marketing and reality in general, or make yourself the fool who believed the photos.

### Variant: the certain narrator (petty grievance)

Some voices are arrogant rather than anxious: the narrator is sure, indignant, and building a legal case over something tiny (a seat map, a queue, a sign, a policy). The comedy works when:
- the grievance is small and the argument is huge,
- the narrator is specifically wrong, and the comeuppance lands on him,
- the target is a rule, a system, or his own plan, never a person or a place.

Escalate in steps, each one a little more reasonable-sounding and a little more insane. Then let reality ruin the case in one plain sentence. This is self-deprecation in a loud costume, so the "narrator is the idiot" rule still holds.

## 6. Comic threes

Two items set a pattern, the third breaks it. The third item should be shorter, odder, or more specific than the first two.

- "I packed sunscreen, a phrasebook, and unearned confidence."
- "We saw a cathedral, a castle, and the inside of a pharmacy."

Rules:
- Make the first two plausible and similar in kind. The third turns left.
- Don't use a three because you reached for a rhythm. If the third item is just another normal item, cut to two.
- Don't stack threes. One or two per post. More and the pattern becomes the tell.

## 7. Punchline placement

The funny word goes last. Rewrite the sentence until it does.

- Weak: "A goat ate my map, which was frankly rude."
- Better: "I was done with that map, but the goat was not."
- Strongest: reorder so the surprising noun is the final word: "The last thing I saw of my map was the goat."

Habits:
- Cut anything after the laugh. No "which was funny", no "I mean", no explanation.
- Short sentence after a long one often hits hardest, but only when the short one carries the joke.
- A paragraph can end on its best line, but don't end every paragraph on a one-liner. It becomes a drumbeat. Sometimes put the joke mid-paragraph and keep moving.

## 8. Asides and parentheticals

A parenthetical is the narrator leaning over to whisper. Use it for the thought the sentence wasn't allowed to say.

- "We walked to the viewpoint (a word I now use loosely)."

Use sparingly: two or three per post. Don't parenthesize a whole paragraph. Fourth-wall breaks ("you're probably thinking I should have checked the map") work once, when they preempt the reader's exact objection. More than once is a tic.

## 9. Joke density and the sincere beat

Roughly one real laugh per paragraph. Not every sentence, and not every paragraph at the same temperature. Let some paragraphs just tell the story and set up the next one.

Reserve room for at least one sincere moment: the view that was actually beautiful, the stranger who actually helped, the meal that was actually great. Write it plainly, with no joke in the last sentence to rescue the narrator from feeling something. A sincere beat lands harder after laughs because the reader has relaxed.

Pacing pattern that works: funny open, escalating mishap, sincere turn, one last twist or callback.

### When something really went wrong

Cancelled flight, illness, a terrible hotel, a scare. The humor can stay, but the aim changes.
- Lead with the plain fact. Joke second.
- Aim at the narrator's reaction and at institutional absurdity (the hold music, the form), not at anyone who was sick, hurt, or frightened.
- Skip jokes that make light of an injury or illness itself while it is happening. After the fact, the narrator can joke about their own behavior in it.
- Let one sincere paragraph carry the weight: what it actually felt like, who helped.
- If the notes don't say how it ended, ask. Don't invent the resolution.

## 10. Never punch down

The joke is on the narrator, the situation, or the absurdity of tourism itself. It is never on:
- locals, a culture, an accent, a language, religion, or poverty
- someone's body, disability, or ethnicity
- a companion's real vulnerability (the voice profile lists off-limits topics)
- a named small business doing its job

If you notice the laugh depends on "those people are strange" or "this place is backward", rewrite so the narrator is the one who was out of place. Foreignness is the narrator's problem, not the place's.

Mocking tourist behavior, including the narrator's own, is fair game. So is institutional absurdity (airline policies, booking systems) as long as no individual worker is the target.

### Borrowing from a beloved band, show, or book

If the voice profile lists influences the author loves, a short allusion can add warmth: a song title bent to fit the moment, a catchphrase, a famous line.
- Keep it short: a title or a few words. Do not reproduce lyrics, verses, or passages. It's a nod, not a copy.
- Fit it to the situation. The reference should be funnier because the moment really is that situation.
- One or two per post. Don't explain them. Readers who get it enjoy it, and the rest should still enjoy the sentence.
- Skip it when it would be forced. A good plain sentence beats a strained reference.

## 11. What not to do

- **Forced puns in every headline.** One good title beats a pun parade. A pun is fine when it also says where the post is.
- **"Hilarious" announcing itself.** Don't write "you won't believe how funny this was". Show it or cut it.
- **Dad-joke stacking.** One groaner can charm. Four in a row read like a sitcom laugh track.
- **Random non sequiturs.** Absurdity works when it's attached to a real detail. "Anyway, llamas" is not a joke.
- **Chatbot sass.** "Spoiler: it did not go well" and "Plot twist:" are stock phrases that appear in every AI-written humor post. See `anti-slop.md`.
- **Over-explaining.** If you have to explain it, cut it or rebuild it.
- **Therapy-speak jokes** ("my inner child had questions"). They date quickly and sound generic.
- **Every paragraph ending in a zinger.** Vary the landings.
- **Inventing a character** ("my wise old taxi driver") to deliver a punchline. Only use people in the notes.
